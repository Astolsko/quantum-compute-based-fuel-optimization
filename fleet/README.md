**A new problem formulation, in its own package.** Phases 1-3 (parent
directory, untouched) optimized a *single voyage* — and honestly concluded
that exact dynamic programming solves that problem outright. This package
moves to where the genuine combinatorial difficulty lives for the Indian
Coast Guard use case: **fleet operations planning**.

> *K vessels, M missions with time windows, a moving weather field.
> Which ship takes which missions, in what order, timed how — to minimize
> total fleet fuel?*

That is assignment × sequencing × routing (VRP-class, NP-hard). The exact
subset DP here is exponential in the number of missions: it is the ground
truth up to 16 missions (about 25 s at 5 ships × 16 missions) and out of reach
beyond. Past that, only a designed heuristic can plan.

**Bottom line of the 2026-10-28 scale study** (10 instances per size, 3 × 6
to 5 × 16, exact optimum every time; tables and tests in
[Results](#results-10-instances-per-size-every-run-counted)):

- **Up to 16 missions, use the exact DP.** At 5 × 16 it takes at most 25 s,
  no longer than the GA (25 s) and half the hybrid's time (53 s). Every
  heuristic there averages at least 3.6% above the optimum, and none finds the
  optimum on more than 1 of 9 instances.
- **Beyond that, use ALNS.** ALNS and the GA are statistically level at every
  size (p ≥ 0.1), but ALNS is faster at every size, 60× at 5 × 16 (0.4 s vs
  25 s).
- **The QUBO hybrid did not hold up at scale.** It is optimal on every 3 × 6
  instance, and at every size its first 25 proposals beat 25 from random
  assignment or the GA. But it stops improving. From 4 × 10 the GA beats it (p ≤ 0.027), and from 5 × 14
  ALNS does too (p ≤ 0.02). Its one useful role is as a starting plan for ALNS
  (`hybrid+alns`): the lowest mean gap at 5 × 16 (+3.6%), but not
  significantly better than ALNS alone at any size.

## Architecture: a matheuristic (exact inner, adaptive outer)

```
            OUTER: adaptive search (ALNS)            <- heuristic, combinatorial
   assignment of missions to ships + serving order
                        |
                 asks, thousands of times
                        v
            INNER: exact voyage oracle               <- optimal, physics-driven
   time-expanded DP over (cell, slot) x speeds
   on the MOVING weather field, costed by the
   Phase-1 ML fuel model (imported read-only)
```

The outer search never wastes effort on badly-sailed voyages: every candidate
plan is priced with *provably optimal* routes and speed profiles inside it.

### The key data structure: Pareto serve tables

`serve[origin, mission, release_slot, finish_by_slot]` — minimum fuel to be
released at a location, sail to the mission, start within its window, and be
**done by** a given slot. The 4th axis carries the **fuel-vs-finish-time
trade-off**: sailing a mission slowly is cheap but can block the ship's next
window. Collapsing this axis (min-fuel only) made real instances look
infeasible — the Phase-1 fuel/time tension reappearing at fleet level is a
central finding of this package.

### The optimizer (`matheuristic.py`)

ALNS-style adaptive search (Ropke & Pisinger 2006 family), with the design
rules our Phase-2/3 benchmarks forced:

- **Feasibility-preserving representation** — plans are (assignment,
  sequence) structures, never penalty-weighted bits. There is *no penalty
  weight to tune* (the knob that wrecked BGSA and QAOA in Phase 2/3).
- **Operator portfolio** — relocate (random/worst), swap, intra-ship
  reorder, and a noisy destroy-and-repair (removes 2-3 missions, reinserts
  regret-style) that can discover coordinated multi-mission exchanges.
- **Adaptive operator weights** — selection probabilities follow recent
  success (the "adaptive" idea, deployed where it can win).
- **Reheating** — temperature restarts from the incumbent after stagnation.
- **Robust construction** — deterministic greedy, then randomized-restart
  greedy: tight-but-feasible instances where plain greedy dead-ends are real
  (seed 1 here).

### The quantum-classical hybrid (`hybrid.py`)

This is the clean way to **employ a quantum technique** here: keep the exact
classical oracle as the inner layer, and let a **QUBO decide the outer
*assignment*** — which ship gets which missions. The QUBO is quantum-portable
(solved with `neal` now; the same QUBO runs on a real annealer/QAOA by swapping
the sampler). The flow is **quantum proposes, classical verifies**:

1. Build a QUBO over `x[ship, mission]` — one-hot per mission, with *exact*
   solo and pairwise bundle costs from the oracle.
2. The sampler returns **many candidate assignments** (its natural output).
3. The oracle scores each **exactly** (best per-ship ordering) and keeps the
   real winner. Overloaded proposals (a pairwise QUBO can't see that a 3+
   mission bundle is jointly time-infeasible) are minimally **repaired** first.

**Why the QUBO does only assignment:** its objective is pairwise, but a ship's
true bundle cost is *stateful* (depends on order, weather, timing). So
sequencing stays in the exact inner layer, where per-ship it's small enough to
solve exactly. The QUBO handles the one genuinely combinatorial, quantum-
friendly decision.

**Result.** On 2026-10-06, at 3 ships × 6 missions, the hybrid was optimal on
10/10 instances against 8/10 for ALNS. Replacing the QUBO with random
assignments, scored the same way, gave 5/10. The 2026-10-28 rerun (new fuel
model, same instances) confirms the small-size result. The hybrid is optimal on
10/10, and its first 25 proposals already contain the optimum on all 10
instances, against 2/10 for 25 random ones (p = 0.008). **The scale study does
not confirm it beyond that.** At every size the QUBO's first 25 proposals are
better than 25 random or GA ones, but more samples barely improve them. From 4 × 10 the GA
overtakes it, and from 5 × 14 ALNS does too, both with p < 0.05. The pairwise
QUBO cannot see what makes a bundle of 3+ missions expensive, and resampling
the same energy does not fix that. Numbers and reasons in
[Results](#results-10-instances-per-size-every-run-counted).

### Hybrid v2: lazy cuts + a stronger repair (`hybrid.solve_hybrid_cuts`)

The v1 hybrid above was optimal on every 3 × 6 instance and **found no feasible
plan at all on 2 of 3 instances at 4 ships × 12 missions** (measured 2026-10-28,
36-slot horizon). The diagnosis is in the formulation: the QUBO sees missions in
pairs, so it cannot see that 3+ missions are jointly impossible for one ship, and
it rated overloaded 5-7-mission bundles *lower* than the true optimum. More reads
cannot fix a mis-specified energy. v2 keeps the same QUBO and adds:

- **Lazy cuts.** Every proposed bundle that turns out infeasible yields a
  *minimal* infeasible subset (deletion filter -- valid because feasibility is
  downward-closed: dropping a mission never makes a ship later, since waiting at
  stations is free). That subset is forbidden by a higher-order penalty,
  quadratised with auxiliary variables (Rosenberg 1975), and the QUBO is resampled.
  Every round is still a plain QUBO -- still quantum-portable.
- **A repair** that strips each overloaded ship to what fits and reinserts the
  stripped missions where they cost least (v1's moved one mission at a time and
  gave up when that was not enough).
- `hybrid+alns`: v2's best plan as ALNS's starting point -- does a QUBO warm start
  help the classical search?

A per-ship capacity constraint was tried too and rejected: it constrained almost
nothing (every ship's true cap was 5, optima use up to 5) and its dense penalty
made v2 worse in 5 of 6 test runs.

### The classical rival: a genetic algorithm over assignments (`genetic.py`)

One gene per mission (which ship serves it); uniform random start (so generation
0 *is* the random ablation), tournament selection, uniform crossover, mutation,
elitism, and Lamarckian write-back of the repaired assignment. It, `rand+exact`
and hybrid v2 propose assignments through the **same** `AssignmentScorer` (same
repair, same exact best order per ship) and are charged per **distinct exact
scoring**, with the best-so-far recorded after every one -- so the three are
compared at every equal budget, not only the final one.

### The earlier QUBO experiment (`qubo_layer.py`) — kept as a finding

Before the clean assignment split, we tried encoding the *whole* plan
(assignment + sequencing) as one QUBO. Two encodings, both instructive:

1. **Dispatch encoding** (mission × ship × release-slot): *structurally fails* —
   location-stateless, so second-wave missions are mispriced and the one-hot
   collapses.
2. **Position encoding** (mission × ship × position, TSP-style à la Lucas 2014):
   sequencing becomes representable but deep-chain *timing* can't fit in pairwise
   terms, so it needs heavy repair and still lands at +14% at 3 × 6 and +50-61%
   from 4 × 12 (below).

The gap between `qubo+repair` and `hybrid` **is the lesson**: don't flatten a
stateful problem into one QUBO — decompose, and give the QUBO only the part that
is genuinely a QUBO (the assignment). The 2026-10-28 scale study adds a second
lesson. Once ships carry 3+ missions, even the assignment is only
approximately a QUBO, because its pairwise costs miss what makes a bundle
expensive.

### Running it on a REAL quantum optimizer — QAOA (`quantum_qaoa.py`)

**Important distinction:** the neal hybrid above uses a QUBO but solves it with
*classical* simulated annealing — so it is quantum-*portable*, not quantum-*run*.
`quantum_qaoa.py` closes that gap: it solves the **same assignment QUBO with QAOA**,
a genuine quantum optimization algorithm, and feeds QAOA's candidate assignments
into the same exact oracle. The backend is one argument:

- `--backend aer` — a local statevector **simulator** of a quantum computer.
  Verified end-to-end: on an 8-qubit instance QAOA finds the exact optimum
  (matches neal + exhaustive). Works today, no account.
- `--backend ibm` — a **real IBM quantum processor** via Qiskit Runtime. Needs a
  free IBM Quantum token (`https://quantum.ibm.com/`); the code path is wired and
  waiting for credentials.

```bash
python quantum_qaoa.py --backend aer --ships 2 --missions 4    # simulator, runs now
pip install qiskit-ibm-runtime                                 # once, for real hardware
python quantum_qaoa.py --backend ibm --token YOUR_IBM_TOKEN    # real quantum hardware
```

**Honest expectation:** on these small, noisy-hardware-unfriendly instances QAOA
will most likely do *worse* than neal — near-term quantum is not a performance win
here. The value is a truthful demonstration: the identical QUBO runs on a real
quantum optimizer, judged by the same exact evaluator. We report whatever it
returns.

#### It actually ran on real quantum hardware ✅

We executed it — an 8-qubit instance (2 ships × 4 missions) on **IBM's
`ibm_fez` (a real 156-qubit quantum processor)**, job `d9gjv53sbqfc73eoocmg`:

| Solver | Fuel | Gap |
|--------|-----:|----:|
| QAOA on **real IBM QPU** | 33.49 t | **+0.0%** |
| neal hybrid | 33.49 t | +0.0% |
| exact optimum | 33.49 t | — |

To stay inside the free QPU quota we used the standard near-term recipe:
**optimise the QAOA parameters on the simulator, then run the one optimised
circuit on the QPU** (a single job). The hardware returned 251 distinct
measured bitstrings; the oracle scored them and produced the optimal plan.

**Read this honestly:** at 8 qubits there are only 2⁴ = 16 possible assignments,
so the *exact oracle* — scoring the measured bitstrings — is what guarantees the
optimum; the noisy hardware only had to surface the good assignment somewhere in
its distribution, which it did. So this is a genuine **"it runs on real quantum
silicon, end-to-end"** milestone, **not** evidence that quantum out-optimises
classical (it doesn't, at this scale). The scientific test — does the QPU's
proposal quality beat classical as the problem grows — needs larger instances and
is future work.

The hardware run decoded the QPU's measured counts directly, so it was not
affected by the sample-dropping bug described in the main README (that bug was
in qiskit-optimization's `MinimumEigenOptimizer`, which the simulator path used
until 2026-10-28).

qubits it is +4.2%, against +0.25% for neal. The GPU simulator makes these

Nine or ten feasible instances per size (one per size from 4 × 10 has no
feasible plan at all, and is excluded). What the tables say, in order of
confidence:

**1. The exact DP is cheap enough to be the planner.** Its time per instance:
at most 0.1 s up to 4 × 10, 0.5 s at 4 × 12, 3.5 s at 5 × 14, 25 s at 5 × 16
(~1.6 GB). At 5 × 16 that is about as fast as the GA and twice as fast as the
hybrid, and every heuristic is worse. Heuristics are only needed beyond 16 missions, where the DP
runs out of memory. There, no exact reference exists to measure them against.

**2. Hybrid v2 fixes v1's failure, but not the ranking.** v2 beats v1 at every
size from 4 × 8 (p ≤ 0.04; 6-14 points of mean gap). But from 4 × 10 it is
no better than random assignment with the same exact scoring (p = 0.055,
0.13, 0.82, 0.13). The GA beats it from 4 × 10 (p = 0.027, 0.004, 0.020,
0.004), and ALNS beats it at 5 × 14 and 5 × 16 (p = 0.020, 0.004).

**3. Why the hybrid stalls.** The QUBO is good at *first* guesses: at 25
scorings it is ahead of random/GA at every size (5 × 16: +28.9% vs +49.9%).
After that it hardly improves. From 200 to 1,000 scorings it gains 0.4-2.3
points at 4 × 12 and above, while the GA gains 15-18 points (4 × 12:
16.9% → 2.1%). It is not running out of proposals. Measured on three instances
each at 4 × 8, 4 × 10 and 4 × 12, v2 spends all 1,000 scorings and adds 75-400
cuts.
The cuts only forbid *infeasible* bundles, though. A feasible bundle that the
pairwise energy underprices stays attractive, so the sampler keeps returning
variants of the same wrong region. The GA's selection is driven by the true
exact cost, so it keeps moving.

**4. GA and ALNS are level; ALNS is far cheaper.** No GA-vs-ALNS difference is
significant at any size (p ≥ 0.11). The GA has the lower mean gap up to 4 × 12,
ALNS from 5 × 14. ALNS takes 0.1-0.4 s; the GA 0.3-25 s.

**5. The QUBO's useful role is as a warm start.** `hybrid+alns` (ALNS started
from v2's best plan) has the lowest mean gap at 4 × 8 (+0.1%) and 5 × 16
(+3.6%, the only method to find a 5 × 16 optimum), and is within 3 points
of the best elsewhere. It is not significantly better than ALNS alone at any size
(p ≥ 0.125), and it costs v2's time (53 s at 5 × 16 against 0.4 s).

**6. ALNS's adaptive operator weights do not pay off here.** The fixed
portfolio has the lower mean gap at every size from 4 × 10 (4.5 vs 4.8, 4.1
vs 7.0, 3.8 vs 5.6, 5.3 vs 6.7%). The adaptive version is ahead only at 3 × 6 and 4 × 8.
Not tested pairwise; it is a reason to look, not a conclusion.

Also visible: greedy dispatch is 18-32% off at every size from 4 × 8, and the
whole-plan QUBO (`qubo+repair`) is the worst structured method throughout
(+50-61% from 4 × 12, with a few instances where repair finds no plan).

## Worked example plans (what the optimizer actually produces)

`run_fleet.py` saves a two-panel plot per instance. **How to read them:**

- **Left — the map.** Background is the region's wave field at mid-horizon
  (pale yellow = calm, dark red = storm core). ★ stars on the west edge are
  the three ships' home ports. Each arrow is a mission leg (start → end),
  coloured by the ship the optimizer assigned it to.
- **Right — the schedule (Gantt).** Each thick bar is a mission occupying its
  ship from `release → finish` slot; consecutive bars on one row are that
  ship's serving order. The thin bar under each row is that mission's allowed
  **start window** `[es, ls]`. Bars are drawn from the *true backtracked
  schedule* (the timeline that actually achieves the optimum), so bar
  boundaries are the real hand-off times, not a per-mission approximation.

Every title shows `ALNS total` next to `exact`. In this run they match on all
three demo instances, and so do the GA and both hybrids.

**Seed 0 — a clean 3-ship split (20.86 t).** The cheap Interceptor (green)
runs a 3-mission chain M4 → M2 → M3 (hand-offs at slots 15 and 22), the Fast
Patrol (orange) takes M5 → M1 (hand-off at 25), and the big Offshore Patrol
(blue) takes the single eastbound M0. Greedy insertion lands 12.6% above this.
(On 2026-10-06, under the older fuel model, M0 and M5 were the other way
round: a different model prices the same legs differently, and the optimum
moves with it.)

**Seed 1 — the instance greedy *fails* (20.91 t).** Greedy cheapest-insertion
dead-ends: it paints itself into a corner and cannot place the last mission
(the `–` in the table below). Every structured method finds a feasible plan,
and ALNS, the GA and both hybrids find the optimum: the Fast Patrol threads
M0 → M4 → M5 between the two storm cores (hand-offs at 13 and 20), the
Interceptor covers M2 → M3 along the southern edge, and the Offshore Patrol
takes M1 in the calm north. Fleet fuel planning is a combinatorial problem,
not something a dispatch rule handles.

**Seed 2 — leave a ship in port (15.70 t).** The Offshore Patrol row is
empty: the optimum deploys two of the three ships. With all six missions east
of a strong central storm, loading the Fast Patrol (M0 → M2 → M5) and the
Interceptor (M4 → M3 → M1) with three missions each is cheaper than sailing
the heavy OPV out of port at all. Greedy, which puts the OPV to work, is 65%
above the optimum. "Don't deploy that ship" is a fleet-level decision a
per-voyage optimizer cannot express. This plan is the same as on 2026-10-06.

## Honest limitations

- Synthetic instances; physics coefficients inherited from Phase 1
  (plausible, not calibrated). The scenario schema is deliberately shaped so
  real vessel/tasking/forecast data can drop in.
- Slot quantisation (1 h) caps effective transit speed at one cell per slot
  — fast ships gain fuel efficiency but not schedule time; finer slots would
  fix this at ~linear table cost.
- Waiting is free only at stations (anchorage assumption); no loitering
  costs mid-ocean, no return-home leg, single region-wide current.
- Missions are point-to-point patrol legs; area-coverage patrols, refuelling
  and crew constraints are future work.
- The exact subset DP is the ground truth up to 16 missions (5 × 16: ~25 s,
  ~1.6 GB). Beyond that there is no optimum to measure against; larger
  instances would need best-known references or a CP-SAT / MIP bound.
- The horizon grows with missions per ship (`fleet_config.horizon_slots`), but
  the generator still makes some instances with no feasible plan at all (they
  are counted and excluded, never hidden).
- Ten instances per size is enough to see large effects, not small ones: a
  difference of 1-2 gap points between two good methods usually comes out
  p > 0.1 in the paired tests. Read the p-values before reading a ranking.
- The hybrid's QUBO is solved by `neal` (classical simulated annealing on the
  Ising form) everywhere except the QAOA script. "Quantum" here means the
  formulation is portable to quantum hardware, not that the plans came from it.

## Where this goes next

- **Give the QUBO what it cannot see.** The oracle already knows the true
  cost of every bundle it scores. Feed that back: reprice (not just forbid)
  bundles whose true cost is above the pairwise estimate, or seed the GA with
  the QUBO's first proposals (the QUBO is good early, the GA late). Either is a
  test of whether a quantum-portable formulation can keep up past 4 × 10.
- **Beyond 16 missions:** no exact reference. A CP-SAT or MIP model gives a
  lower bound to measure ALNS against.
- **ALNS operator weights:** the fixed portfolio was as good or better from
  4 × 10. Either tune the adaptation (segment length, reaction factor) or drop it.
- **A faster benchmark:** the 6-size study took 65 min on one core; instances
  are independent, so it parallelises (`--jobs`, as the route benchmark has).
- **Real quantum hardware at a size that matters:** the IBM run was 8 qubits,
  where scoring all 16 assignments is exhaustive. 18-24 qubits now simulate in
  seconds, so a hardware run at 3 × 6 or 4 × 6 is the next meaningful test.
- **Real data drop-in:** IMD/INCOIS gridded forecasts + actual vessel/tasking
  lists into the existing scenario schema.
